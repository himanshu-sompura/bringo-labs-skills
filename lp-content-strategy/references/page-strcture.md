# Landing page structure

Use after discovery, before drafting a full page or changing its section order. Start with the entry message, what the visitor already knows, the action they’re being asked to take, and the biggest reason a qualified buyer may hesitate. Preserve any approved section order. Flag a placement change if the order buries information the buyer needs.

## Choose the order by what could stop the buyer

Use **offer → strongest unresolved doubt → remaining decision details → action** as a starting point. The first screen should identify the offer and give ready visitors a direct route to the action. Use the next block for the answer most likely to earn another scroll.

| What the visitor needs next | What to put early |
| --- | --- |
| “Is this the exact offer I clicked for?” | Put the advertised deliverable, price or date where it applies, and the matching action in the hero. For a simple download or signup, the form can sit there if visitors already know what they’ll receive. |
| “I can see the promise. How would this work for me?” | Show the key step, sample deliverable, or product view just below the hero. Put the evidence beside the explanation it supports. |
| “I understand how it works. Can I trust this?” | Bring the closest relevant result, customer example, or demo forward. Keep enough context to interpret it in the same block. |
| “Can I afford it, qualify, or make the timing work?” | Move the price or pricing basis, eligibility, or start date up. Keep the decisive condition visible before the form or purchase step. |

Combine blocks that answer the same doubt. Split one when buyers need to find two different answers independently, such as eligibility and price. Give a section its own space only when it adds evidence, resolves a separate doubt, or enables the action. Repeat the primary CTA after a block that gives buyers a new reason to act and near the end of a long page. Each repeat should lead to the same next step.

## Make the page easy to scan on a phone

- **Lay out the first two screens together.** Give the offer and action priority over a large image, header, or badge row. Let the next heading or a clear continuation cue show that there’s more to see. Check this on the rendered page when available.
- **Group what belongs together.** Keep the heading closer to its own text than to the block above. Keep proof captions with their evidence and action notes with their buttons. Stack adjacent columns on mobile in the order the buyer needs them.
- **Choose the layout by the job.** Use one reading column for an argument, numbered steps for a sequence, and side-by-side content for a real comparison. On mobile, keep the information needed to compare items together. Give each section one element to notice first.
- **Keep key answers visible.** Use an accordion for optional details that only some buyers need. Put the price, key terms, and crucial proof in the main flow when they determine whether the buyer acts.

## Check the outline before drafting

Write one line per block with **buyer question → answer or evidence → placement**. Skim those lines in mobile order. Move any answer that comes after the step that depends on it. Trim duplicate blocks before shortening a block that resolves a real concern.

**Example for a cold B2B service page**  
Offer and CTA → sample deliverable with a caption → relevant result and context → price and client responsibilities → application. If the real hesitation is price, put the pricing basis alongside the offer and keep the full terms near the application.

## Research behind the guide

- Nielsen Norman Group eye-tracking research supports prioritizing the first screen, giving headings visual prominence, and grouping them with their content. [Scrolling and attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/) · [How visitors scan headings](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/)
- Google Ads recommends carrying the ad’s offer and action onto the page. MarketingExperiments documents a case where moving the answer to a specific buyer concern improved results. [Google Ads guidance](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6238826?hl=en) · [Buyer concern and placement test](https://marketingexperiments.com/conversion-marketing/testing-for-anxiety-in-product-pages)
- GOV.UK’s layout and accordion guidance supports a single-column starting point on small screens and keeps essential information visible. [Layout](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/styles/layout/) · [Accordion guidance](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/accordion/)
- Treat the sequences above as starting points. Page-length experiments test specific offers and audiences. Use completed purchases, qualified bookings, or the campaign’s equivalent to judge an actual change. [Page-length test for a specialized product](https://marketingexperiments.com/copywriting/long-copy-vs-short-copy-tested) · [Email-signup test with inconclusive results](https://www.nextafter.com/experiments/how-shortening-the-length-of-landing-page-copy-impacted-email-conversions/)
