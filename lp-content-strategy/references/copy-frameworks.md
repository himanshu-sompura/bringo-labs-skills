# Landing page copy frameworks

Use after discovery, while drafting or fixing a weak section. Start with the entry message and the buyer’s biggest unresolved question. Apply only the patterns that help answer it. Keep framework names out of the page copy.

## 1. Choose the lead by what the buyer knows

| What the visitor arrives knowing                 | How to write the opening                                                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| They feel the problem                            | **PAS** — Name the trigger, show one credible consequence, then introduce how the offer helps. Keep the consequence proportional to the evidence.               |
| They know the outcome or are comparing solutions | **Outcome → mechanism** — Lead with the result or capability they want. Use the subhead to name the offer and explain the concrete step that makes it possible. |
| They came for a specific offer                   | **Offer → decision detail** — Name the offer first and surface the detail they need to act, such as price, eligibility, or start date.                          |

Carry the specific promise from the ad, search intent, or outreach into the first screen. If the headline needs context, let the subhead supply it.

## 2. Turn the difference into a benefit

**Buyer’s current alternative → distinct capability → practical payoff.** Name what they actually use or do today, including doing it manually. Identify a relevant difference in the offer, then ask, “What can the buyer now see, do, or decide?” Write that answer. If it still sounds generic, add the specific step or deliverable that enables it.

## 3. Match the evidence to the doubt

| Buyer’s doubt                                   | What to put beside the claim                                                       |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Will this work in my situation?                 | A relevant result with its metric, timeframe, and buyer context, when available.   |
| What will I actually get?                       | A demo, sample, or annotated screenshot. Label illustrative examples.              |
| What will it cost me in money, time, or effort? | The confirmed price or requirement, including who does the work when that matters. |

When evidence is limited, narrow the claim to what it supports. A sample report can show what gets tracked. A result claim needs measured outcomes.

## 4. Carry the action through

**Button → immediate next step → confirmation.** Label the real action. Add the one detail that resolves hesitation at the click, such as a form before booking or when a reply is due. After the action, confirm what happened and give the next step or verified timing.

## Example

Fictional invoice reporting tool for finance teams comparing software.

- **First draft** — Streamline accounts receivable with powerful reporting.
- **Headline** — See which invoices are overdue before your Monday review.
- **Subhead** — Connect your billing account to get a weekly report grouped by customer and days overdue.
- **CTA** — Preview the sample report
- **Proof caption** — Sample report with fictional invoice data. See how unpaid balances are grouped by customer and days overdue.

## Ten landing-page content principles

Use these checks when planning, writing, or auditing a page. Start with the doubt most likely to stop a good-fit visitor. For cold traffic, show why the offer matters, how it works, and why it’s credible before asking for a commitment. Treat conversion lift as a hypothesis to test.

### 1. Continue the promise that earned the click

Read the ad or outreach message, hero, and CTA together. They should address the same buyer, need, and outcome. Carry the incoming promise into the headline, explain the offer underneath, and give visitors the next step they expect. Use separate versions when campaigns make substantially different promises.

### 2. Name what made the buyer look for help

Look for four facts in buyer conversations. What happened? What did they try? Where did it fall short? What do they want now? Use language buyers repeat and verify wording and permission before quoting. If the trigger is still a guess, note the assumption in the handoff and open with the facts of the offer.

### 3. Make headings explain the offer

Read only the headings and buttons in mobile order. Can you tell what buyers get, how it works, why they should believe it, and what they can do next? Rewrite generic labels so they give an answer. “Track enquiries through to paid admissions” tells buyers what they’ll learn from the section.

### 4. Explain what produces the benefit

Show what you do, what it changes, and why that matters to the buyer. For example, “We add qualification questions to the enquiry form so your admissions team can spot applicants who meet your programme requirements.” Introduce a branded method name once the work is clear.

### 5. Put evidence beside the claim it supports

Show a short demo to support ease of use. Name the person and their responsibilities to support a founder-led delivery claim. For a case study, give the baseline, result, timeframe, and any context or limits that affect how the buyer reads it. A rise in enquiries supports an enquiry claim. An enrollment claim needs enrollment data.

### 6. Answer objections where they arise

Put the answer beside the line that raises the doubt. Show inclusions, extra charges, and commitment near pricing. Explain what the buyer must provide or handle beside the process. Put measurement limits beside results. Use the FAQ for questions that still need an answer.

### 7. Make the next step clear at the CTA

Say what starts when the buyer clicks, what they’ll receive, and any meaningful time, cost, or commitment. Check that the form, booking, and confirmation copy carry through on that expectation. For a form before scheduling, say “Share your programme details, then choose a time to speak with Himanshu.”

### 8. Match the ask to what the buyer knows

List what a first-time visitor needs to know before acting. For a service, that may include scope, price, proof, and who does what. Give ready buyers an early route and put the remaining decision details before the later CTA. Use urgency only for real timing constraints.

### 9. Cut vague wording and keep buying details

Replace abstractions with work buyers can picture. “Connect your ads, course page, and admissions follow-up” makes the scope clear. Keep the conditions, evidence, and terms that affect the decision. After shortening, check that readers can still tell what’s delivered and who does it.

### 10. Give each section one buying question

Finish this sentence for each section. “After reading this, the visitor should understand or believe ___.” Merge sections that give the same answer. Cut sections that add no useful information or evidence. Repeat the primary CTA after a section gives buyers a new reason to act.

### Prioritize and measure

Check message match, proof, and CTA clarity first. Fix the issue with the clearest evidence. Compare results by traffic source where possible. Track clicks and submissions to see where people drop off, then use qualified enquiries and sales to judge whether the change helped the business.
